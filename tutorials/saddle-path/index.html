Finding the saddle path

Which initial choice leads to equilibrium? A phase diagram and a shooting algorithm, one guess at a time.

Explore the model ↓Download notebook ↗

The intuition

Initial capital is inherited. Initial consumption is chosen. A household that consumes too much uses up the economy’s capital; one that consumes too little accumulates capital while sacrificing consumption. Equilibrium requires a particular initial consumption choice that takes the economy toward its steady state.

This is a saddle path. Convergence occurs along a narrow set of initial choices. Nearby guesses can initially look similar and later move apart. That makes a phase diagram useful, and makes unrestricted forward simulation numerically delicate.

The model

The resource constraint and Euler equation give two laws of motion:

k˙=Akα−c−δk,c˙c=αAkα−1−δ−ρσ.\dot k=Ak^\alpha-c-\delta k, \qquad \frac{\dot c}{c}=\frac{\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1}-\delta-\rho}{\sigma}.k˙=Akα−c−δk,cc˙​=σαAkα−1−δ−ρ​.

Capital kkk and consumption ccc are per worker. Productivity is AAA, depreciation is δ\deltaδ, impatience is ρ\rhoρ, and σ\sigmaσ controls the willingness to substitute consumption across time. Population is constant in this example.

The capital nullcline is c=Akα−δkc=Ak^\alpha-\delta kc=Akα−δk: capital does not change along this curve. Below it, capital rises; above it, capital falls. The consumption nullcline is the vertical line

k∗=(αAδ+ρ)1/(1−α).k^*=\left(\frac{\alpha A}{\delta+\rho}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}.k∗=(δ+ραA​)1/(1−α).

To its left, consumption rises; to its right, consumption falls. Their intersection is (k∗,c∗)(k^*,c^*)(k∗,c∗).

Pause & predict

Why can we choose c₀ but not k₀ when finding the equilibrium path?

Discuss the answer

Capital is a state inherited from the past. Consumption is a control chosen now. The initial consumption choice must respect both the initial capital stock and the long-run equilibrium condition.

Explore the mechanism

Try a low consumption guess and a high one. Then click the bisection button: test the midpoint, classify the failure, and keep the half of the bracket that still contains opposite failures. The slider can inspect any guess; it does not change the bisection bracket.

The guess is too low: capital overaccumulates and consumption eventually vanishes. Keep the upper half when this is the midpoint test.

α = ⅓, δ = 0.05, ρ = 0.03, σ = 2, A = 1. The phase diagram shows at most 120 years; the failure test uses up to 400. Numerical error eventually pushes a saddle trajectory away from its stable path.

One step of shooting

Once the endpoints fail in opposite ways, the next numerical step is small:

midpoint = (low + high) / 2
failure = classify_path(midpoint)

If the midpoint drains capital, lower the upper endpoint. If it overaccumulates capital and consumption vanishes, raise the lower endpoint. Repeat the same test on the smaller interval.

The bracket is information about the initial choice. It is not a collection of feasible equilibrium trajectories.

Why the plotted horizon matters

The stable path has an unstable direction nearby. Small errors from finite precision, integration or the initial guess can eventually push a simulated path into that direction. A trajectory that behaves well for a finite period is evidence about the guess; it is not proof of exact infinite-horizon convergence.

Use the informative early part of the path to explain the economics, and use tolerances and opposite-failure brackets to explain the numerical method.

Pause & predict

A trial path stays close to the steady state for a long time and then departs. Has the economic saddle path disappeared?

Discuss the answer

No. The numerical path may have picked up a small component in the unstable direction. Increasing accuracy extends the useful simulation horizon, but forward shooting remains sensitive.

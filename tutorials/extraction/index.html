A stock, a price, a choice

Does a bigger stock change the extraction fraction? Homogeneity, stochastic prices and the cost of capacity.

Explore the model ↓Download notebook ↗

The intuition

A resource owner trades revenue today against the option to sell the remaining stock later. Today’s price matters, and so does the distribution of tomorrow’s price.

Now imagine doubling the stock and doubling extraction. If every payoff doubles as well, scale changes the size of the problem without changing its proportions. The optimal extraction fraction can stay the same at every stock level.

An additional quadratic capacity cost changes this argument. Doubling extraction quadruples that cost. Proportional extraction becomes more expensive for a larger owner, so stock size matters for the optimal fraction.

Pause & predict

With no capacity cost, does a flat q/S curve mean every owner extracts the same amount?

Discuss the answer

No. The same fraction applied to a larger stock gives a larger quantity. Scale independence concerns the fraction, not the level of extraction.

Explore the mechanism

Set capacity cost to zero and compare stocks. Add capacity cost while holding today’s price fixed. Then change the price and discuss both the incentive to sell now and the value of waiting.

Experiment / Test the scaling argument

The fraction falls from 25.4% at the smallest plotted stock to 12.7% at the largest. Quadratic capacity cost breaks proportional scaling.

β = 0.95, log-price persistence ρ = 0.8, innovation standard deviation 0.1. These are precomputed grid policies with V(0,p) = 0. Changing today's price also changes the distribution of expected future prices.

A small expected-value step

On a stock-by-price grid, each row of the transition matrix PPP is a conditional distribution of tomorrow’s price. The continuation-value calculation is

expected_value = value @ transition.T
next_stock = stock - extraction

The second line usually lands between stock nodes, so the solver interpolates. Including the zero-stock boundary prevents numerical truncation from being mistaken for a failure of homogeneity.

What to take away

A scaling argument can remove a state variable before computation begins. When a new cost has a different degree of homogeneity, that shortcut may fail. The plot tests the economic argument; the grid and boundary conditions determine how accurately the numerical calculation reproduces it.

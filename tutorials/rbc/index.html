How local is a local approximation?

When do two decision rules stop agreeing? Numerical and log-linear RBC policies facing the same shock.

Explore the model ↓Download notebook ↗

The intuition

A numerical decision rule can follow the curvature of a model across its state space. A first-order approximation describes the model locally, using a linear rule around the steady state. Close to that point, the two can agree remarkably well.

The useful experiment is to give both rules the same shock and the same initial capital, then enlarge the shock. Differences in their paths then reflect differences in the decision rules, rather than differences in the realised productivity sequence.

The model

The RBC household chooses consumption CCC, hours NNN and next-period capital K′K'K′ subject to

C+K′=ZKαN1−α+(1−δ)K.C+K'=ZK^\alpha N^{1-\alpha}+(1-\delta)K.C+K′=ZKαN1−α+(1−δ)K.

Productivity evolves according to

log⁡Zt+1=ρlog⁡Zt+σεt+1,εt+1∼N(0,1).\log Z_{t+1}=\rho\log Z_t+\sigma\varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad \varepsilon_{t+1}\sim\mathcal N(0,1).logZt+1​=ρlogZt​+σεt+1​,εt+1​∼N(0,1).

The discount factor is β\betaβ, α\alphaα is the capital share, δ\deltaδ depreciation, ρ\rhoρ productivity persistence and σ\sigmaσ the standard deviation of productivity innovations. The model also values leisure; labor supply and consumption are chosen jointly with saving.

The numerical solution supplies a policy K′=g(K,Z)K'=g(K,Z)K′=g(K,Z). The log-linear solution uses hats for log deviations from the steady state:

k^t+1=akk^t+azz^t,k^t=log⁡(Kt/Kˉ).\hat k_{t+1}=a_k\hat k_t+a_z\hat z_t,\qquad \hat k_t=\log(K_t/\bar K).k^t+1​=ak​k^t​+az​z^t​,k^t​=log(Kt​/Kˉ).

Pause & predict

If both paths differ, could that simply be because one received a larger productivity shock?

Discuss the answer

That would make the comparison uninformative. Here the productivity path is deliberately identical under both rules, and both start at steady-state capital.

Explore the mechanism

Start with the one-percent log-productivity shock. Increase its size while keeping everything else fixed. The plot shows capital relative to the steady state; the reported gap compares the two capital levels directly.

For this small shock, the two paths are very close. A local approximation captures the main response.

β = 0.99, α = 0.36, δ = 0.025, ρ = 0.95, σ = 0.007. One initial innovation; subsequent innovations are zero. The 20% log shock is a deliberate stress test. The gap is max |Kⁿᵘᵐ − Kˡⁱⁿ| / Kˡⁱⁿ, expressed as a percent.

The numerical simulation receives that same productivity level and applies its own capital policy. Each rule updates from the capital it generated in the preceding period.

What the comparison tells us

As the shock grows, the economy moves farther from the point at which the linear rule was derived. Curvature in the numerical policy becomes more important. The largest shock is a deliberate stress test, many innovation standard deviations from a typical shock in this calibration.

A small discrepancy for ordinary shocks supports using the local approximation for the question being asked. It does not guarantee accuracy for large shocks or every statistic.

What it does not isolate

A first-order approximation is certainty equivalent: its coefficients do not capture how shock variance changes decisions through risk. The numerical policy solves the stochastic model, so its differences can include risk effects and numerical approximation error as well as curvature.

Holding the realised path fixed and increasing its size primarily illustrates the loss of local accuracy. Identifying precautionary saving separately would require an experiment that varies uncertainty, or a higher-order solution.
